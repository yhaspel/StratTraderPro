Interpreting PBO

PBO stands for Probability of Backtest Overfitting. It answers one blunt question: when you pick the best-looking parameters on historical data, how often do those exact parameters turn out to be a poor choice going forward? It's a single number between 0 and 1, and it's shown per symbol on the tearsheet.

The short version. Low PBO = the parameters that looked best in-sample also tended to do well out-of-sample, so your selection process is finding something real. High PBO (above 0.5) = the best in-sample parameters tended to underperform out-of-sample, which is the classic signature of overfitting — you tuned to noise.

Why it exists

Any backtest that sweeps many parameter combinations has a built-in trap: with enough combinations, something will look great on your history by pure luck. Pick that winner and you've optimized to noise. A big total return and a high Sharpe don't tell you whether you fell into this trap. PBO is designed to tell you exactly that.

How it's computed (CSCV)

PBO uses CSCV — Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (Bailey et al., 2015). In plain terms:

  1. Every parameter combination is run over the full date range, giving a matrix of daily returns (one column per combination).
  2. The timeline is chopped into equal blocks, and those blocks are split many different ways into an "in-sample" half and an "out-of-sample" half.
  3. For each split, it finds the combination that was best in-sample, then checks where that same combination ranked out-of-sample.
  4. PBO is the fraction of splits where the in-sample winner landed in the bottom half out-of-sample.

If your selection had real predictive power, the in-sample best would keep ranking well out-of-sample and PBO would be low. If it was luck, the in-sample best lands anywhere out-of-sample — including the bottom half about half the time — and PBO climbs toward and past 0.5.

How to read the number

PBOReading
0.0 – 0.2Low overfitting risk. The selected parameters generalize well.
0.2 – 0.5Moderate. Some overfitting; weigh it against per-window Sharpe stability and drawdown.
> 0.5High overfitting risk — the tearsheet shows a warning badge. The in-sample-best parameters tend to underperform out-of-sample. Treat the results as unreliable.
A high total return with PBO above 0.5 is a red flag, not a green light. It means the impressive number is likely an artifact of picking the luckiest combination, and there's little reason to expect it to repeat.

When PBO is blank (null)

PBO needs enough parameter combinations and enough history to be meaningful. If the sweep had too few combinations (fewer than 10) or the date range was too short for the block splits, PBO is left unset and the tearsheet notes "insufficient trials for an overfit estimate." That's not a pass or a fail — it just means the test couldn't run. To get a PBO, widen the parameter grid and/or lengthen the date range.

What to do about a high PBO

  • Simplify the strategy or the grid. Fewer parameters and fewer combinations give overfitting less room to hide.
  • Lean on the robustness signals, not the headline. Prefer a strategy with low PBO and steady per-window Sharpe over one with a bigger return and a high PBO.
  • Lengthen the history. More out-of-sample data makes the estimate stronger and makes it harder to fit noise.

PBO doesn't replace judgment — but a strategy that clears a low PBO, holds a stable per-window Sharpe, and survives its own drawdown is a far better candidate than one that just posted a big backtest number. See also Reading the tearsheet.
